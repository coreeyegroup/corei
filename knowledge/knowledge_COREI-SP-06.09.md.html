1. PURPOSE

The COREI Specifications Evolution document defines
the authoritative lifecycle through which
institutional specifications are

created,

reviewed,

approved,

implemented,

validated,

changed,

versioned,

deprecated,

superseded,

retired,

and historically preserved.

Its purpose is to ensure that COREI specifications
can evolve without creating

architectural drift,

implementation ambiguity,

conflicting authority,

silent incompatibility,

uncontrolled change,

knowledge loss,

or institutional fragmentation.

Specifications SHALL evolve.

They SHALL NOT drift.

2. SCOPE

This document governs the evolution of

system specifications,

platform specifications,

service specifications,

application specifications,

API specifications,

event specifications,

data specifications,

security specifications,

operations specifications,

runtime specifications,

infrastructure specifications,

integration specifications,

interface specifications,

workflow specifications,

trading specifications,

risk specifications,

portfolio specifications,

execution specifications,

artificial intelligence specifications,

and future institutional specification classes.

This document governs both

individual specification evolution,

and the evolution of the Specifications Domain
itself.

Controlled Evolution

A specification SHALL NOT evolve independently of
the institutional authority above it.

A specification SHALL NOT remain frozen when
institutional reality has legitimately changed.

Evolution SHALL be controlled alignment.

4. SPECIFICATIONS AS LIVING AUTHORITY

Specifications are authoritative implementation
contracts.

They SHALL remain synchronized with approved

institutional intent,

governance,

architecture,

engineering principles,

standards,

implementation requirements,

security requirements,

and operational reality.

A specification that no longer represents approved
institutional reality SHALL be

updated,

deprecated,

superseded,

or retired.

It SHALL NOT remain silently authoritative.

5. EVOLUTION OBJECTIVES

Specification evolution SHALL preserve

authority,

clarity,

compatibility,

traceability,

determinism,

ownership,

implementation alignment,

operational continuity,

historical knowledge,

and institutional coherence.

Evolution SHALL improve the specification system
without weakening its constitutional foundations.

6. EVOLUTION TRIGGERS

Specification evolution MAY be triggered by

new institutional capability,

approved architecture change,

new engineering requirement,

new standard,

implementation evidence,

operational evidence,

security findings,

incident learning,

performance findings,

scalability requirements,

new technology,

provider change,

market evolution,

regulatory or jurisdictional requirements,

deprecation,

or institutional learning.

Every material evolution SHALL have an identifiable
trigger.

7. NO CHANGE WITHOUT REASON

A specification SHALL NOT change merely because

a different style is preferred,

a new technology is fashionable,

an implementation is temporarily inconvenient,

or an individual prefers another design.

Material specification change SHALL be justified
by institutional need and evidence.

8. CHANGE CLASSIFICATION

Specification changes SHALL be classified.

Approved classifications MAY include

Editorial Change

Clarification

Additive Change

Compatible Change

Restrictive Change

Behavioural Change

Breaking Change

Constitutional Change

Deprecation

Supersession

Retirement

The classification SHALL determine the required
review and migration process.

9. EDITORIAL CHANGE

An Editorial Change modifies

formatting,

spelling,

grammar,

layout,

or non-semantic wording

without changing institutional meaning.

Editorial changes SHALL NOT alter

requirements,

authority,

behaviour,

scope,

ownership,

or compliance obligations.

Editorial changes MAY use a lightweight review
process.

10. CLARIFICATION

A Clarification improves understanding without
changing the intended requirement.

A clarification MAY

remove ambiguity,

add explanation,

improve examples,

or make an existing obligation more explicit.

A clarification SHALL NOT silently introduce a new
requirement.

11. ADDITIVE CHANGE

An Additive Change introduces new specification
content without invalidating existing compliant
implementations.

Additive changes MAY define

new optional capability,

new extension point,

new field,

new operational requirement,

or new supported context.

Additive changes SHALL still be reviewed for hidden
compatibility impact.

12. COMPATIBLE CHANGE

A Compatible Change modifies a specification while
preserving compatibility for existing approved
consumers.

Compatibility SHALL be demonstrated.

It SHALL NOT be assumed.

A change described as compatible but requiring
unplanned consumer modification SHALL be
reclassified.

13. RESTRICTIVE CHANGE

A Restrictive Change reduces previously permitted
behaviour.

Restrictive changes MAY be required for

security,

risk,

correctness,

governance,

or operational safety.

Affected consumers SHALL be identified.

Migration requirements SHALL be explicit.

14. BEHAVIOURAL CHANGE

A Behavioural Change modifies the meaning or
runtime behaviour of an existing specification.

Behavioural changes SHALL define

previous behaviour,

new behaviour,

affected capabilities,

migration,

validation,

and operational impact.

Behavioural change SHALL NOT be hidden inside
editorial revision.

15. BREAKING CHANGE

A Breaking Change invalidates one or more existing
compliant consumers or implementations.

Breaking changes SHALL require

explicit justification,

impact analysis,

consumer identification,

migration plan,

version strategy,

validation,

operational readiness,

and approval.

Breaking change SHALL never be introduced silently.

16. CONSTITUTIONAL CHANGE

A Constitutional Change modifies a specification
requirement derived directly from

COREI Identity,

COREI Governance,

COREI Architecture,

COREI Engineering,

or institutional constitutional authority.

Constitutional changes SHALL require the highest
applicable review authority.

A specification document SHALL NOT independently
rewrite constitutional authority.

17. CHANGE REQUEST

Material specification evolution SHALL begin with
an identifiable change request.

A change request SHOULD define

requested change,

reason,

trigger,

affected specification,

affected capabilities,

expected benefit,

known risk,

compatibility impact,

and requesting authority.

The request SHALL be traceable.

18. CHANGE OWNERSHIP

Every material specification change SHALL have an
owner.

The owner SHALL coordinate

analysis,

review,

impact assessment,

drafting,

approval,

migration,

validation,

publication,

and closure.

Change ownership MAY transfer.

Current ownership SHALL remain explicit.

19. IMPACT ANALYSIS

Before material specification change,
the impact SHALL be analyzed.

Impact analysis SHOULD consider

identity,

governance,

architecture,

engineering,

standards,

dependent specifications,

implementations,

APIs,

events,

data,

security,

operations,

documentation,

tests,

deployment,

and consumers.

The absence of immediately visible impact SHALL NOT
be treated as proof of no impact.

20. DEPENDENCY ANALYSIS

Specification evolution SHALL examine dependency
relationships.

Analysis SHALL identify

upstream authorities,

downstream specifications,

implementations,

consumers,

operational procedures,

and historical relationships.

A specification SHALL NOT be changed as though it
exists independently.

21. CONSUMER ANALYSIS

Material changes SHALL identify affected consumers.

Consumers MAY include

services,

platforms,

applications,

modules,

engines,

providers,

workflows,

automation,

operators,

teams,

external integrations,

and future implementations.

Unknown consumers SHALL be treated as a migration
risk.

22. AUTHORITY ANALYSIS

Before changing a specification,
the owner SHALL determine whether the proposed
change is permitted by higher authority.

The analysis SHALL consider

Identity

Governance

Architecture

Engineering

Standards

Existing Specifications

A lower authority SHALL NOT override a higher
authority through specification revision.

23. ARCHITECTURE CHANGE BOUNDARY

If a requested specification change requires an
architecture change,
the architecture SHALL be changed through the
approved architecture process first.

The specification SHALL then be updated to reflect
the approved architecture.

Specification evolution SHALL NOT be used as a
hidden architecture redesign mechanism.

24. STANDARD CHANGE BOUNDARY

If a specification change conflicts with an
authoritative standard,
the conflict SHALL be resolved at the appropriate
authority level.

The specification SHALL NOT silently weaken or
override the standard.

Standards evolution and specification evolution
SHALL remain traceably connected.

25. IMPLEMENTATION FEEDBACK

Implementation experience MAY trigger specification
evolution.

Implementation feedback MAY identify

ambiguity,

impossibility,

unnecessary complexity,

missing requirement,

unsafe assumption,

performance limitation,

or integration conflict.

Implementation feedback SHALL be evaluated.

Implementation convenience alone SHALL NOT
automatically redefine the specification.

26. OPERATIONAL FEEDBACK

Operational evidence MAY trigger specification
evolution.

Evidence MAY include

incidents,

near misses,

capacity limits,

recovery failures,

security findings,

operator difficulty,

alert patterns,

or recurring manual intervention.

Operational reality SHALL inform evolution.

Temporary operational workaround SHALL NOT silently
become specification authority.

27. SECURITY-DRIVEN EVOLUTION

Security findings MAY require accelerated
specification evolution.

Security-driven changes SHALL still preserve

authority,

traceability,

impact analysis,

validation,

and migration

to the extent operational urgency permits.

Emergency security change SHALL be reconciled into
authoritative specifications after immediate risk
is controlled.

28. INCIDENT-DRIVEN EVOLUTION

Material incidents MAY reveal specification defects.

Incident-driven evolution SHALL distinguish between

implementation defect,

operational defect,

architecture defect,

standard defect,

and specification defect.

The specification SHALL change only when the
specification itself requires correction or
improvement.

29. EVIDENCE-DRIVEN EVOLUTION

Material specification evolution SHOULD be
supported by evidence.

Evidence MAY include

tests,

benchmarks,

incident records,

security findings,

operational metrics,

consumer feedback,

implementation results,

market requirements,

or governance decisions.

Evidence SHALL strengthen institutional reasoning.

30. DRAFTING

A proposed specification change SHALL be drafted
clearly.

The draft SHALL distinguish

existing authority,

proposed change,

new requirements,

removed requirements,

migration requirements,

and unresolved questions.

Draft language SHALL avoid accidental ambiguity.

31. NORMATIVE LANGUAGE

Specification evolution SHALL preserve consistent
normative language.

SHALL

indicates a mandatory requirement.

SHALL NOT

indicates a mandatory prohibition.

SHOULD

indicates a strong institutional expectation unless
a justified exception exists.

SHOULD NOT

indicates behaviour generally prohibited unless
explicitly justified.

MAY

indicates an allowed option.

Normative meaning SHALL NOT be changed casually.

32. REVIEW

Material specification changes SHALL undergo review
appropriate to their classification.

Review MAY include

domain owner,

architecture authority,

engineering authority,

security authority,

operations authority,

data authority,

trading authority,

risk authority,

or other affected institutional authority.

Review scope SHALL reflect impact.

33. REVIEW QUESTIONS

Specification review SHOULD determine

Is the change necessary?

Is the authority correct?

Is the requirement clear?

Is ownership explicit?

Is compatibility understood?

Are consumers identified?

Is migration defined?

Is security preserved?

Is operational continuity preserved?

Can compliance be validated?

Is historical traceability preserved?

34. APPROVAL

A specification change SHALL become authoritative
only after approval by the required authority.

Draft publication SHALL NOT equal approval.

Implementation SHALL NOT automatically equal
approval.

Operational use SHALL NOT automatically equal
approval.

Authority SHALL be explicit.

35. VERSIONING

Specifications SHALL use controlled versioning.

Version changes SHALL communicate the significance
of evolution.

The versioning model MAY distinguish

major,

minor,

and patch

changes.

Version semantics SHALL remain consistent across
the Specifications Domain.

36. MAJOR VERSION

A major version SHOULD change when evolution
introduces

breaking change,

major behavioural change,

major authority change,

or substantial specification restructuring.

Major version change SHALL require explicit
migration consideration.

37. MINOR VERSION

A minor version SHOULD change when evolution
introduces

compatible new capability,

new requirement,

material clarification,

or additive institutional behaviour

without requiring a major compatibility break.

38. PATCH VERSION

A patch version SHOULD change for

editorial correction,

non-semantic repair,

minor clarification,

or equivalent low-impact change.

Patch versions SHALL NOT hide behavioural change.

39. VERSION IMMUTABILITY

An approved published version SHALL be treated as
historically immutable.

Corrections SHALL produce a new version.

Historical versions SHALL NOT be silently rewritten
in a manner that destroys traceability.

40. EFFECTIVE VERSION

Every governed specification SHALL have one clearly
identifiable effective authoritative version for a
given institutional context.

Multiple historical versions MAY exist.

Authority SHALL not be ambiguous.

41. VERSION METADATA

Specification metadata SHOULD identify

document identity,

version,

status,

authority,

owner,

dependencies,

supersession,

and repository location.

Metadata SHALL support machine and human
traceability.

42. STATUS MODEL

Specifications MAY use lifecycle statuses such as

Draft

Under Review

Approved

Active

Deprecated

Superseded

Retired

Archived

Status semantics SHALL be consistent.

A specification SHALL NOT be represented as
Approved when approval is incomplete.

43. DRAFT

Draft specifications are non-authoritative unless
explicitly designated otherwise.

Drafts MAY support

design,

review,

experimentation,

and discussion.

Production compliance SHALL NOT depend upon an
unapproved draft without explicit temporary
authority.

44. UNDER REVIEW

A specification Under Review has entered formal
evaluation but is not yet authoritative as the new
effective version.

The currently effective approved specification
SHALL remain authoritative until superseded or
otherwise governed.

45. APPROVED

An Approved specification has received required
institutional authority.

Approval SHALL identify the version that was
approved.

Approval SHALL NOT automatically mean all
implementations have completed migration.

46. ACTIVE

An Active specification is the effective
specification governing applicable implementation
and operations.

Where Approved and Active are distinct statuses,
the activation condition SHALL be explicit.

47. DEPRECATED

A Deprecated specification remains temporarily
available but SHOULD NOT be used for new
implementation.

Deprecation SHALL define

reason,

replacement,

migration path,

support period,

and retirement condition.

Deprecation SHALL be visible.

48. SUPERSEDED

A Superseded specification has been replaced by a
newer authoritative specification.

The superseded document SHALL identify its
replacement.

The replacement SHALL identify the superseded
document where appropriate.

Superseded specifications SHALL remain available
for historical traceability.

49. RETIRED

A Retired specification no longer governs active
institutional implementation.

Retirement SHALL occur only after

applicable consumers have migrated,

required historical evidence is preserved,

and authority has transferred or ended.

Retired specifications SHALL not remain hidden
active dependencies.

50. ARCHIVED

Archived specifications are preserved for
historical,
legal,
audit,
research,
or institutional knowledge purposes.

Archived status SHALL clearly indicate that the
document is not active authority.

51. DEPRECATION PROCESS

Specification deprecation SHALL define

deprecated specification,

reason,

replacement,

affected consumers,

migration requirements,

support period,

risk,

owner,

and retirement condition.

Deprecation SHALL be governed.

It SHALL NOT be abandonment.

52. DEPRECATION PERIOD

The deprecation period SHALL reflect

consumer count,

migration complexity,

risk,

operational impact,

security impact,

and institutional urgency.

Security-critical or unsafe specifications MAY
require accelerated retirement.

53. SUPERSESSION PROCESS

Supersession SHALL create an explicit authority
transition.

The new specification SHALL define

what it replaces,

effective version,

compatibility impact,

migration,

and activation condition.

The old specification SHALL identify the
superseding authority.

54. RETIREMENT PROCESS

Before specification retirement,
the owner SHALL confirm

no active governed consumer requires the retired
authority,

migration is complete,

required documentation is updated,

required tests are updated,

required operations are updated,

and historical evidence is preserved.

Retirement SHALL be evidence-based.

55. MIGRATION

Material specification evolution SHALL define
migration when existing consumers are affected.

Migration SHALL define

source state,

target state,

affected consumers,

sequence,

compatibility period,

validation,

operational risk,

and completion criteria.

Migration SHALL not depend upon undocumented
consumer behaviour.

56. MIGRATION OWNERSHIP

Every material migration SHALL have an owner.

The specification owner MAY coordinate migration.

Consumer owners SHALL remain responsible for their
implementations.

Migration responsibility SHALL not become
ambiguous between specification and implementation
owners.

57. MIGRATION PLAN

A migration plan SHOULD identify

consumer inventory,

priority,

dependency order,

required changes,

testing,

deployment,

rollback or forward recovery,

validation,

and completion evidence.

High-risk migration SHALL be staged where
appropriate.

58. COMPATIBILITY PERIOD

A specification MAY support multiple versions
during controlled migration.

The compatibility period SHALL define

supported versions,

consumer expectations,

end date or exit condition,

and retirement process.

Indefinite multi-version support SHALL require
explicit institutional justification.

59. BACKWARD COMPATIBILITY

Backward compatibility SHALL be preserved where
institutionally valuable and technically sound.

Compatibility SHALL NOT override

security,

correctness,

risk,

or constitutional authority.

Compatibility requirements SHALL be explicit.

60. FORWARD COMPATIBILITY

Specifications SHOULD support safe evolution where
future extension is reasonably foreseeable.

Forward compatibility MAY use

extension fields,

version negotiation,

optional capabilities,

or explicit extensibility points.

Speculative complexity SHALL be avoided.

61. DUAL-RUN

High-risk specification migrations MAY use
controlled dual-run.

Dual-run MAY compare

old behaviour,

new behaviour,

outputs,

performance,

or operational characteristics.

Dual-run SHALL define

authority,

comparison criteria,

duration,

and cutover condition.

Two competing authoritative outcomes SHALL NOT
remain unresolved.

62. SHADOW VALIDATION

A new specification implementation MAY operate in
shadow mode before becoming authoritative.

Shadow execution SHALL not produce unauthorized
external effects.

Shadow results MAY support

comparison,

validation,

capacity analysis,

and operational confidence.

63. CUTOVER

Specification migration cutover SHALL define

authority,

time or condition,

consumer readiness,

dependency readiness,

operational readiness,

validation,

and recovery.

Cutover SHALL be explicit.

Authority SHALL NOT silently shift through partial
deployment.

64. POST-CUTOVER VALIDATION

After cutover,
the new specification implementation SHALL be
validated.

Validation SHOULD confirm

correct behaviour,

consumer compatibility,

security,

performance,

operational health,

data integrity,

and institutional outcomes

where applicable.

Migration SHALL remain incomplete until required
validation succeeds.

65. ROLLBACK

Where rollback is safe,
migration SHALL define rollback conditions.

Rollback SHALL consider

specification version,

implementation version,

data,

state,

events,

external effects,

and consumer compatibility.

Rollback SHALL NOT be assumed safe after
irreversible evolution.

66. FORWARD RECOVERY

Where rollback is unsafe,
migration SHALL define forward recovery.

Forward recovery MAY include

corrective specification revision,

implementation correction,

state migration,

data repair,

or consumer migration.

The recovery strategy SHALL be explicit for
high-risk evolution.

67. VALIDATION OF EVOLUTION

Specification evolution SHALL be validated.

Validation MAY include

document validation,

dependency validation,

compatibility validation,

implementation validation,

integration validation,

security validation,

performance validation,

operational validation,

and migration validation.

Approval without validation SHALL NOT prove
successful evolution.

68. COMPLIANCE TRANSITION

When a specification changes,
compliance expectations SHALL define a transition.

The transition SHALL identify

effective version,

affected capabilities,

migration deadline or condition,

temporary compatibility,

and enforcement.

Consumers SHALL not be declared non-compliant
without an applicable effective requirement.

69. GRANDFATHERING

Existing implementations MAY be temporarily
grandfathered only through explicit authority.

Grandfathering SHALL define

scope,

reason,

risk,

owner,

conditions,

and expiration.

Permanent undocumented grandfathering is
prohibited.

70. EXCEPTION DURING EVOLUTION

Temporary exceptions MAY be required during
migration.

Every exception SHALL define

affected requirement,

reason,

scope,

risk,

owner,

compensating controls,

monitoring,

and expiration.

Migration exception SHALL NOT become permanent
unmanaged divergence.

71. TRACEABILITY

Specification evolution SHALL preserve traceability
between

change request,

decision,

version,

approval,

implementation,

migration,

validation,

and operational outcome.

A qualified reviewer SHALL be able to reconstruct

what changed,

why it changed,

who approved it,

and what was affected.

72. CHANGE HISTORY

Every specification SHOULD maintain a meaningful
change history.

The history SHOULD identify

version,

date,

change classification,

summary,

and authority

where appropriate.

Change history SHALL communicate semantic evolution.

It SHALL not become meaningless administrative
noise.

73. DECISION RECORD

Material specification evolution SHOULD reference
the decision that authorized it.

The decision record MAY exist in

governance documentation,

architecture decision records,

engineering decisions,

security decisions,

or specification change records.

The specification SHALL remain connected to its
reasoning.

74. HISTORICAL PRESERVATION

Superseded and retired specifications SHALL be
preserved where required for

audit,

incident investigation,

historical reconstruction,

research,

or institutional learning.

Historical preservation SHALL distinguish old
authority from current authority.

75. NO SILENT REWRITE

Approved specification history SHALL NOT be
silently rewritten.

Material correction SHALL create a traceable new
version.

Historical integrity is an institutional
requirement.

76. NO PARALLEL AUTHORITY

Two conflicting specifications SHALL NOT both
remain silently authoritative for the same scope.

Where multiple versions are temporarily supported,
the authority and compatibility model SHALL be
explicit.

Conflict SHALL be resolved.

77. NO ORPHAN SPECIFICATION

Every active specification SHALL have

an owner,

an authority chain,

a defined scope,

and identifiable consumers or intended capability.

Unowned active specifications are prohibited.

78. NO ORPHAN CONSUMER

Material specification evolution SHALL seek to
identify active consumers.

A consumer that depends upon obsolete authority
SHALL be

migrated,

excepted,

deprecated,

or retired.

Hidden consumer dependency is an institutional
risk.

79. NO IMPLEMENTATION-ONLY AUTHORITY

Implementation SHALL NOT silently redefine an
approved specification.

If implementation reveals a better institutional
model,
the specification SHALL be formally evolved.

Code behaviour alone SHALL NOT become
constitutional specification authority.

80. NO DOCUMENTATION-ONLY AUTHORITY

A specification SHALL NOT claim operational
authority when production reality materially
contradicts it.

The contradiction SHALL trigger

implementation correction,

specification evolution,

or explicit exception.

Documentation and reality SHALL be reconciled.

81. NO PATCH EVOLUTION

Specification evolution SHALL correct the proper
authority layer.

If the defect originates in

architecture,

the architecture SHALL be corrected.

If the defect originates in

standards,

the standard SHALL be corrected.

If the defect originates in

specification,

the specification SHALL be corrected.

If the defect originates in

implementation,

the implementation SHALL be corrected.

A lower layer SHALL NOT permanently patch a defect
owned by a higher layer.

82. EVOLUTION OF API SPECIFICATIONS

API specification evolution SHALL explicitly govern

versioning,

compatibility,

consumer migration,

field evolution,

error evolution,

authentication,

authorization,

and retirement.

Breaking API changes SHALL not be introduced
silently.

83. EVOLUTION OF EVENT SPECIFICATIONS

Event specification evolution SHALL explicitly
govern

schema compatibility,

producer migration,

consumer migration,

ordering,

semantics,

replay,

retention,

and versioning.

Event schema compatibility SHALL be validated
against actual consumers.

84. EVOLUTION OF DATA SPECIFICATIONS

Data specification evolution SHALL govern

schema,

meaning,

lineage,

quality,

migration,

retention,

and historical compatibility.

Changing field meaning without explicit versioning
or migration is prohibited.

85. EVOLUTION OF SECURITY SPECIFICATIONS

Security specification evolution SHALL prioritize

risk reduction,

identity integrity,

least privilege,

confidentiality,

integrity,

availability,

and institutional continuity.

Security evolution MAY require accelerated
migration.

Urgency SHALL not eliminate traceability.

86. EVOLUTION OF OPERATIONS SPECIFICATIONS

Operations specification evolution SHALL be driven
by

production evidence,

incidents,

capacity,

recovery experience,

operator experience,

and institutional growth.

Operational evolution SHALL reduce unmanaged manual
dependency and improve institutional control.

87. EVOLUTION OF TRADING SPECIFICATIONS

Trading specification evolution SHALL preserve

market authority,

signal authority,

decision authority,

risk authority,

portfolio authority,

execution authority,

position authority,

and reconciliation.

Trading changes SHALL not collapse approved
authority boundaries.

88. EVOLUTION OF RISK SPECIFICATIONS

Risk specification evolution SHALL preserve the
independence and authority of risk controls.

Risk changes SHALL define

affected limits,

decision behaviour,

failure behaviour,

migration,

and validation.

Trading convenience SHALL not silently weaken risk
authority.

89. EVOLUTION OF EXECUTION SPECIFICATIONS

Execution specification evolution SHALL preserve

order identity,

account context,

broker context,

strategy context,

portfolio context,

risk authority,

execution state,

and reconciliation.

Execution changes SHALL prioritize safety and
determinism.

90. EVOLUTION OF AI SPECIFICATIONS

Artificial intelligence specification evolution
SHALL distinguish

probabilistic capability,

deterministic institutional authority,

tool authority,

data authority,

and operational control.

New model capability SHALL NOT automatically expand
institutional authority.

91. EVOLUTION OF INFRASTRUCTURE SPECIFICATIONS

Infrastructure specification evolution SHALL
preserve

availability,

security,

recoverability,

operational control,

and platform compatibility.

Infrastructure modernization SHALL not silently
break dependent institutional capabilities.

92. EVOLUTION ACROSS MULTI-CONTEXT SYSTEMS

Specification evolution SHALL consider COREI’s
multi-context institutional requirements.

These include

Multi Account

Multi Broker

Multi Strategy

Multi Portfolio

Multi Asset

Multi Region

Multi Currency

Multi Market

Multi Team

Multi Role

Multi Monitor

Multi Custodian

Multi Exchange

Multi Jurisdiction

24x7 Operations

High Scale Decision Making

Institutional Governance

Professional Operational Workflows

A change valid for one context SHALL not be assumed
valid for all contexts.

93. SCALE-DRIVEN EVOLUTION

Growth in

users,

services,

events,

data,

accounts,

brokers,

strategies,

portfolios,

regions,

or operational complexity

MAY require specification evolution.

Scale-driven evolution SHALL preserve architecture
boundaries and deterministic behaviour.

94. TECHNOLOGY-DRIVEN EVOLUTION

New technology MAY justify specification evolution
when it materially improves institutional
capability.

Technology adoption SHALL be evaluated against

architecture,

security,

reliability,

operability,

migration,

cost,

and institutional longevity.

Technology novelty alone SHALL NOT justify change.

95. PROVIDER-DRIVEN EVOLUTION

External provider change MAY require specification
evolution.

Provider-driven change SHALL remain isolated behind
approved provider boundaries where architecture
permits.

External provider behaviour SHALL NOT directly
rewrite internal institutional authority.

96. REGULATORY AND JURISDICTIONAL EVOLUTION

New legal,
regulatory,
market,
or jurisdictional obligations

MAY require specification evolution.

Such evolution SHALL identify

affected jurisdiction,

affected capability,

effective requirement,

implementation impact,

and operational impact.

Jurisdiction-specific change SHALL preserve
institutional coherence.

97. EVOLUTION CADENCE

Specifications MAY evolve

continuously,

periodically,

or event-driven

according to institutional need.

No fixed cadence SHALL prevent urgent necessary
change.

No rapid cadence SHALL eliminate required
governance.

98. PERIODIC REVIEW

Critical specifications SHOULD undergo periodic
review.

Review SHOULD determine whether the specification

remains authoritative,

remains accurate,

remains necessary,

reflects current architecture,

reflects operational reality,

and requires evolution.

No change is a valid review outcome.

99. STALENESS

Specification staleness SHALL be treated as an
institutional risk.

Staleness MAY be indicated by

contradiction with architecture,

contradiction with implementation,

contradiction with operations,

obsolete dependencies,

unowned requirements,

or unresolved exceptions.

Stale specifications SHALL be reviewed.

100. EVOLUTION BACKLOG

Proposed specification improvements MAY be
maintained in a governed evolution backlog.

The backlog SHOULD identify

proposal,

reason,

priority,

owner,

impact,

and status.

Backlog presence SHALL NOT imply approval.

101. PRIORITIZATION

Specification evolution SHALL be prioritized
according to institutional impact.

Priority MAY consider

security,

financial risk,

trading risk,

operational risk,

architecture integrity,

consumer impact,

regulatory urgency,

and strategic value.

Documentation convenience SHALL not determine
institutional priority.

102. EVOLUTION GOVERNANCE

Specification evolution SHALL remain governed.

Governance SHALL ensure

correct authority,

appropriate review,

explicit approval,

traceable change,

controlled migration,

and historical preservation.

Governance SHALL support evolution.

It SHALL not exist merely to delay change.

103. AUTOMATED VALIDATION

Where practical,
specification evolution SHOULD use automated
validation.

Automation MAY verify

metadata,

references,

dependencies,

schema,

links,

naming,

versioning,

compatibility,

or implementation conformance.

Automation SHALL support institutional judgement.

It SHALL NOT replace authority.

104. MACHINE-READABLE SPECIFICATIONS

Specifications MAY include machine-readable forms
where useful.

Machine-readable forms MAY support

validation,

generation,

testing,

policy enforcement,

or integration.

The relationship between human-readable and
machine-readable authority SHALL be explicit.

105. GENERATED ARTIFACTS

Artifacts generated from specifications SHALL be
traceable to

source specification,

version,

generation process,

and resulting artifact.

Generated artifacts SHALL NOT become independent
uncontrolled authority.

106. SPECIFICATION REGISTRY

COREI SHOULD maintain an authoritative registry of
specifications.

The registry SHOULD identify

document identity,

title,

domain,

version,

status,

owner,

dependencies,

supersession,

and location.

The registry SHALL support institutional
discoverability.

107. DEPENDENCY GRAPH

COREI SHOULD maintain visibility into specification
dependencies.

The dependency graph SHOULD support

impact analysis,

change planning,

migration,

and retirement.

Dependency visibility SHALL reduce hidden
institutional coupling.

108. CONSUMER REGISTRY

Critical specifications SHOULD identify known
consumers where practical.

Consumer visibility SHALL support

impact analysis,

migration,

compatibility,

and retirement.

The registry MAY be automated from repository,
runtime,
or deployment evidence.

109. EVOLUTION EVIDENCE

Material specification evolution SHALL preserve
evidence.

Evidence MAY include

change request,

review,

approval,

impact analysis,

migration plan,

test results,

deployment results,

operational validation,

and retirement confirmation.

Evidence SHALL support future reconstruction.

110. EVOLUTION METRICS

COREI MAY measure specification evolution.

Metrics MAY include

change frequency,

breaking change frequency,

migration duration,

exception duration,

stale specification count,

consumer migration status,

and retirement completion.

Metrics SHALL support improvement.

They SHALL NOT incentivize unnecessary change.

111. EVOLUTION DEBT

Specification evolution debt SHALL be identified.

Evolution debt MAY include

stale documents,

unresolved contradictions,

indefinite deprecations,

unmigrated consumers,

unknown dependencies,

or missing historical traceability.

Evolution debt SHALL not remain invisible.

112. EVOLUTION INCIDENT

A failed specification evolution MAY itself create
an institutional incident.

Examples include

breaking consumer compatibility,

incorrect migration,

authority ambiguity,

security regression,

operational failure,

or data corruption.

Such incidents SHALL follow the appropriate
institutional incident lifecycle.

113. EVOLUTION ROLLBACK OF AUTHORITY

In exceptional circumstances,
institutional authority MAY revert to a prior
specification version.

Authority rollback SHALL be explicit.

It SHALL define

reason,

effective version,

consumer state,

implementation state,

data or state compatibility,

and recovery.

Authority SHALL not oscillate ambiguously.

114. EMERGENCY EVOLUTION

Emergency specification evolution MAY occur when
delay creates unacceptable

security,

financial,

trading,

operational,

or institutional risk.

Emergency evolution SHALL preserve,
as far as practical,

ownership,

authority,

traceability,

validation,

and post-change review.

Emergency change SHALL be reconciled into the
normal specification system.

115. TEMPORARY SPECIFICATION

A temporary specification MAY be created for a
time-bounded institutional need.

Temporary specifications SHALL define

purpose,

scope,

owner,

authority,

effective period,

replacement or retirement condition,

and risk.

Temporary SHALL NOT become permanent through
inaction.

116. EXPERIMENTAL SPECIFICATION

Experimental specifications MAY support controlled
research or validation.

Experimental specifications SHALL define

scope,

environment,

authority,

limitations,

success criteria,

and exit condition.

Experimental specifications SHALL NOT silently
govern production.

117. SPECIFICATION FORK

Specification forks SHOULD be avoided.

Where multiple variants are legitimately required,
the relationship SHALL be explicit.

Variants SHALL define

shared authority,

divergent scope,

compatibility,

and ownership.

Uncontrolled forks create institutional
fragmentation.

118. SPECIFICATION MERGE

Multiple specifications MAY be consolidated when
their authority or scope has become unnecessarily
fragmented.

Consolidation SHALL preserve

requirements,

history,

consumer impact,

and migration.

Merge SHALL not silently discard valid authority.

119. SPECIFICATION SPLIT

A specification MAY be divided when its scope has
become too broad.

The split SHALL preserve

authority,

ownership,

dependencies,

consumer mapping,

and historical traceability.

Division SHALL improve clarity without creating
gaps.

120. SPECIFICATION RESTRUCTURING

Structural reorganization SHALL distinguish between

content relocation,

authority change,

and semantic change.

Moving content SHALL NOT accidentally change its
authority.

Semantic changes SHALL be reviewed independently
of formatting or repository restructuring.

121. REPOSITORY EVOLUTION

Specification repository structure MAY evolve.

Repository evolution SHALL preserve

document identity,

discoverability,

references,

history,

and authority.

A file move SHALL NOT create a new institutional
specification unless explicitly intended.

122. NAMING EVOLUTION

Specification naming MAY evolve when institutional
terminology changes.

Renaming SHALL preserve traceability.

Old names SHOULD redirect or reference the new
authority where practical.

Renaming SHALL not hide semantic change.

123. OWNERSHIP EVOLUTION

Specification ownership MAY transfer as
institutional responsibilities evolve.

Ownership transfer SHALL preserve

authority,

knowledge,

open changes,

migration responsibilities,

and historical traceability.

Unowned transition periods are prohibited.

124. DOMAIN EVOLUTION

The Specifications Domain itself MAY evolve.

New specification categories MAY be introduced
when institutional capability requires them.

Domain evolution SHALL preserve

classification,

naming,

authority,

dependency structure,

and discoverability.

New categories SHALL not duplicate existing
authority without justification.

125. FUTURE SPECIFICATIONS

Future institutional capabilities MAY require new
specifications.

New specifications SHALL derive from

Identity,

Governance,

Architecture,

Engineering,

Standards,

and existing applicable Specifications.

Future capability SHALL extend the institutional
system.

It SHALL NOT bypass it.

126. INSTITUTIONAL LEARNING LOOP

Specification evolution SHALL participate in the
institutional learning loop.

Implementation

↓

Operation

↓

Observation

↓

Evidence

↓

Learning

↓

↓

Specification Evolution

↓

Implementation Improvement

This loop SHALL preserve authority and evidence.

127. KNOWLEDGE PRESERVATION

Specification evolution SHALL preserve not only
the latest requirement,
but the institutional reasoning that produced it.

Knowledge preservation SHALL support

future engineering,

incident investigation,

architecture evolution,

audit,

research,

and institutional continuity.

128. GENERATIONAL CONTINUITY

COREI specifications SHALL be capable of evolving
across generations of

engineers,

operators,

leaders,

technologies,

markets,

providers,

and infrastructure.

Institutional knowledge SHALL outlive individual
participants.

Specification evolution is therefore a continuity
mechanism.

132. EVOLUTION COMPLIANCE

Specification evolution is compliant only when
applicable requirements are demonstrated through
evidence.

Evidence MAY include

change records,

impact analysis,

review records,

approval,

version history,

migration results,

validation results,

consumer status,

and retirement evidence.

A newer document is not automatically a valid
evolution.

133. EVOLUTION VALIDATION QUESTIONS

Every material specification evolution shall answer
the following constitutional questions.

Why is the change required?

What triggered it?

Who owns it?

What authority permits it?

Does it require architecture change?

Does it conflict with a standard?

What specifications depend upon it?

What implementations consume it?

Is the change compatible?

Is it breaking?

What security impact exists?

What operational impact exists?

What migration is required?

How will migration be validated?

What version will become authoritative?

What happens to the previous version?

How will consumers know the authority changed?

Can the change be rolled back safely?

If not,
what is the forward recovery path?

How will historical authority be preserved?

Can another qualified team reconstruct why the
change occurred?

Does the change preserve institutional coherence?

If any mandatory answer is negative,

specification evolution remains incomplete.
